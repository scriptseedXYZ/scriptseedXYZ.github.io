AI assistant for a regulated review workflow

Anonymized · AI Product Management · current
ProblemDomain experts run several review cycles per client submission, and most of that time goes to repetitive verification: digging through historical documents to confirm each error a rules engine had already flagged. Cycles dragged and clients waited. The experts also carry the accountability for every decision, so the hard constraint wasn't cognitive load. It was that experts won't accept an AI insight they can't verify.
DecisionI kept the deterministic rules engine intact and inserted AI only after a flag fires: the engine still detects, the AI generates a cited, explainable insight for each flag. That was a deliberate push against my own team, who wanted AI to replace the engine and do the flagging itself. I argued the deterministic layer was auditable and faster to ship, and moving AI upstream would multiply the trust burden and the audit surface. The architecture followed from that stance: a RAG pipeline on AWS Bedrock over ~9,000 documents with Titan embeddings; Aurora chosen over OpenSearch for our scale and access pattern at materially lower cost; and Claude Haiku 4.5 for generation after a head-to-head evaluation showed the quality delta against frontier models didn't justify roughly 10x the token cost. Per-tenant metadata isolation is enforced at the database layer, non-negotiable for regulated data.
OutcomeIn the pilot cohort, review time dropped about 50% (from 2-3 days to ~1.5 per cycle) at a 75% acceptance rate, rolled out senior-experts-first with a control group left on the old process for comparison. The gate for external rollout is 90% cited relevance across a full cycle. The sharpest lesson came from getting a metric wrong: acceptance rate alone couldn't separate "accepted because correct" from "accepted because rushed," so I'm adding a monthly blind sampled audit to calibrate acceptance against ground-truth correctness: designed in, not retrofitted.

I build AI products for domains where being wrong has consequences: regulated workflows where a human expert's judgment is the accountability layer.

Claims, contracts, submissions, filings: work where a person's name is attached to the decision and a client or regulator can ask them to defend it months later. In these settings the interesting problem isn't making the model better. It's designing the system around it so that when the model is wrong, because it will be, sometimes, the failure is caught, the cost is bounded, and the humans stay in the loop. The AI product isn't the model. It's the system of controls, feedback, and human judgment that surrounds it.

How I think · 01

Trust compounds in small pieces

The instinct is to hand AI the whole job: detect the issue, explain it, present it. In regulated work that's usually the wrong shape. Where a deterministic layer already exists (a rules engine, a validation service), leave it in place and put AI after it: let the rules do detection, let the model do explanation against a corpus the user can verify. You shrink the trust surface. Instead of one large act of faith, you ask for smaller trusts, more of them, each verifiable on its own.

How I think · 02

Guardrails map to failure modes, or they're overhead

Guardrails treated as an end-of-project hygiene layer produce bloat: generic controls that don't obviously map to specific risks, each costing latency, tokens, or user friction. I start from the failure modes instead: what can go wrong, what it costs when it does, and the layered control that makes it acceptably unlikely. Anything that doesn't trace to a specific failure mode isn't a guardrail. It's overhead you're paying for nothing.

How I think · 03

Measure correctness, not acceptance

Acceptance rate, thumbs-down, click-through: easy to measure, and they trend up over time. But they're behavioral metrics, not quality ones. A time-pressured user rubber-stamping a plausible answer looks identical to one who actually verified it, until something breaks. So a ground-truth audit (senior reviewers blind-checking a random sample of accepted outputs) runs alongside from day one, not retrofitted after the client complaint.

Trust Before Throughput

A framework for introducing AI into regulated workflows without losing the humans.

The shape of the problem

A common pattern for AI product managers in regulated domains looks like this: there's a workflow where human experts review something (claims, contracts, submissions, filings) across multiple cycles. Most of the review time is spent on repetitive lookup and verification work that AI could plausibly reduce. Leadership wants faster cycle times. Users want less cognitive load. On paper, this is a straightforward AI opportunity.

In practice, the standard AI PM playbook (pick a model, retrieve some context, generate an output, measure quality) doesn't fully apply. What makes these workflows hard isn't the technical problem. It's that the human experts aren't just labor to be automated. They're the audit and accountability layer. Their names are attached to decisions. When a client challenges a result, or a regulator asks a question, the expert's judgment is what gets defended.

That changes the product problem. The question isn't "can AI do this work faster than a human." It's "can AI produce output that a domain expert can verify faster than they could do the work themselves." Those are different problems. The first is a model quality problem. The second is a product design problem, and it's the one that actually determines whether AI in these domains earns the trust required to keep operating.

What follows is a set of principles I've applied, and, in one case, learned by getting wrong, for building AI products in this shape.

Put AI behind deterministic layers where possible

The first instinct when introducing AI into a review workflow is to use it end-to-end. Ingest the input, identify the issues, explain them, present to the user. One AI system, doing the whole job.

For regulated workflows, this is usually the wrong shape. If there's an existing deterministic component in the workflow (a rules engine, a validation service, a policy check), leave it in place. Insert AI after it, not around it. Let the deterministic layer do detection. Let the AI do explanation, contextualization, and precedent lookup for what the deterministic layer flagged.

Two things happen when you do this. First, you preserve the auditability of the detection step. A rule that fires with a threshold and a condition is defensible in a way that a probabilistic model's flag isn't. Second, and more important, you shrink the trust surface. Instead of asking users to trust that the AI can accurately identify issues and explain them, you ask them to trust only the explanation, against a corpus they can verify.

Trust compounds when you don't ask users to trust everything at once. Ask for smaller trusts, more of them, and layer them so each is verifiable independently.

There are cases where AI has to do detection, where no deterministic layer exists, or where the pattern is too complex for rules. In those cases, the trust work is harder and the guardrail architecture has to carry more weight. But when a deterministic layer already exists, replacing it with AI is trading auditable determinism for probabilistic inference. That trade is rarely worth it in regulated contexts.

Architecture principles that matter more than they look

Two technical decisions come up repeatedly in AI systems of this shape, and both are worth defending consciously rather than defaulting to.

Vector store selection. The default choice for RAG systems in AWS environments is often OpenSearch: it's a strong retrieval engine and it's what teams reach for. But for many enterprise use cases, the corpus is smaller than OpenSearch is optimized for, the retrieval is heavily metadata-filtered, and the access pattern is closer to relational than to full-text search. In those cases, Aurora with vector capabilities is often sufficient, integrates cleanly with the rest of the data layer, and is materially cheaper.

The principle: pick the store that matches the workload, not the one with the strongest theoretical characteristics on a workload you don't have. Running the actual comparison (retrieval quality on your data, at your scale, with your metadata patterns) is worth the week it takes. Most teams don't do this and pay for it in cloud spend.

Model tier selection. The default choice for generation is usually a frontier model. The assumption is that quality will be higher. Often it is, but the delta varies enormously by task. For structured tasks like contextualizing flagged issues against retrieved documents, smaller models can perform within margin of the larger models at roughly an order of magnitude lower cost per inference.

The discipline is running the head-to-head evaluation on your actual task, holding a quality bar, and picking the cheapest model that clears it. This is uncomfortable: it forces you to defend a cost-conscious choice against the assumption that bigger is better, but at production volume, the cost delta between model tiers is not marginal.

One architectural choice that isn't negotiable in multi-tenant regulated contexts: metadata isolation has to be enforced at the database layer, not the application layer. Application-layer filtering means a bug in application logic can leak documents from one client's context into another's. Database-layer filtering means isolation is enforced by the query itself. This distinction matters because the failure mode (cross-client data leakage) is a compliance-grade event, not a UX issue.

Guardrails as failure-mode-mapped controls

Guardrails in AI products are often treated as a hygiene layer added at the end. That framing produces bloat: a list of generic controls that don't obviously map to specific risks, each one costing latency or tokens or user friction.

The framing that works better is to start from failure modes and work backward. For each failure mode: what can go wrong, what's the cost of it going wrong, and what layered controls make it acceptably unlikely?

In regulated review workflows, five failure modes recur:

Cross-client data leakage. The highest-severity failure. Primary control: metadata-scoped retrieval at the database layer. Secondary: audit logging of every retrieval, with post-hoc investigation capability. Tertiary: periodic red-team testing with mismatched metadata to verify the system returns nothing.

Ungrounded inference. The model produces a plausible-sounding output that isn't supported by retrieved documents. The output looks correct, which is what makes it dangerous. Primary control: strict citation requirement in the system prompt. Secondary: escalation as a first-class output when retrieval returns nothing sufficient. The model must be able to say "I don't have enough to answer this" without treating it as a failure state.

Automation bias. The user accepts an inference because it's plausible, not because they verified it. This survives all the technical guardrails, because it's a human failure enabled by product design. The control isn't technical. It's structural. Design the output so verification is faster than fabrication would be worthwhile. Every inference includes the citation the user can click through to. The retrieved document surfaces alongside the inference. Rejecting or escalating is a single click. Make verification the path of least resistance.

Latency and throughput degradation. Users lose trust in the tool's reliability when it falls behind. Controls are conventional but need to be defined: SLA monitoring at p95 and p99, batch processing where real-time isn't required, rate limiting against API caps, graceful degradation states.

Cost blowout. Batch inference at scale can produce surprising bills. Controls: per-tenant cost budgeting, monitoring on tokens per request, and alerting on deviation from historical patterns.

Two guardrails are worth flagging as product decisions rather than engineering choices:

Temperature at near-zero for generation. In regulated contexts, consistency and grounding matter more than creativity. Two similar inputs should produce similar outputs, not stylistic variations. This isn't a hyperparameter choice. It's a product stance about what kind of AI this is.

Human review as non-negotiable. There should be no configuration where AI output is auto-applied to consequential artifacts. The expert is always the last mile. This isn't a v1 constraint you plan to relax. It's the shape of the product.

Every guardrail costs something. The PM job isn't to add guardrails. It's to defend the specific set of guardrails as worth their cost. Anything that doesn't trace to a specific failure mode isn't a guardrail; it's overhead.

Sequencing trust in rollout

Shipping the system isn't the launch. Getting the users to trust it enough to catch its errors, rather than rubber-stamp them, is.

Rollout in regulated workflows should be sequenced deliberately. The first cohort should be senior users, not junior ones. This is counterintuitive because senior users are usually the harder sell. But they're the right early users for two reasons. They can recognize when the AI's output is subtly wrong, because they've built the intuitions the model is trying to approximate. And they have the standing to criticize the tool credibly. If a senior user says an output is misleading, that feedback carries weight. If a junior user says the same thing, it might be read as unfamiliarity.

Starting with the population that can most credibly criticize the system produces better signal than starting with the population most likely to accept it.

Feedback loops should be structured, not ambient. Thumbs-down with required comments. Weekly triage that classifies each rejection into buckets: retrieval problem, generation problem, or process problem. Each bucket routes to a different team. The classification discipline matters more than the volume of feedback: it forces the loop to stay closed, and it gives users visible evidence that their feedback matters.

Expansion gates should be defined upfront. Internal expansion has one bar; external client-facing rollout has a higher one. Holding those gates against pressure to lower them is one of the harder product decisions on this class of project, and one worth committing to publicly early.

The metric trap

There's one lesson I've learned by getting it wrong on projects of this shape, and it's the most consequential one I'd pass on.

Behavioral metrics from users (acceptance rate, thumbs-down rate, click-through) are easy to measure, directional, and often trend upward over time. It's tempting to anchor on them as the primary signal that a product is working.

They're not quality metrics. They're behavioral metrics. And the gap between the two matters more than it looks.

High acceptance rate can mean the AI's outputs are correct and users are validating them. It can also mean users are time-pressured, the output looks plausible, and accepting is the path of least resistance. Both patterns produce the same number.

For any AI product where the user's own workflow depends on trusting the output, behavioral acceptance is a lagging indicator of quality, and worse, it's ambiguous. Users who under-trust the system eventually stop using it, which does show up in the metric. But users who over-trust the system look identical to users who correctly trust it, until something goes wrong.

The correction is a ground-truth check running in parallel from day one. A sampled audit where senior users re-verify a random subset of accepted outputs, blind to the fact that they were accepted. If audit agreement diverges from acceptance rate, that's the signal that the behavioral metric is misleading.

When the headline metric for an AI product is a behavioral signal, there needs to be a correctness check running alongside it from launch, not retrofitted later.

In regulated domains, the cost of measuring engagement instead of correctness doesn't show up in the behavioral metric until it shows up somewhere much worse: a client complaint, an audit finding, a regulatory question.

Closing

There are several ways to solution AI into a regulated workflow. Speed to production, aggressive scale, and non-disruptive parallel deployment are all legitimate paths, and each produces a different product. The path I've argued for here prioritizes earning trust before earning throughput: AI as a bounded assistant behind a deterministic layer, in a workflow the users already understand. It costs some speed and some ambition. What it produces is a system users adopt, correct, and eventually ask to expand, the point at which internal rollout becomes external rollout, and the users who pushed back hardest at the start start surfacing new use cases where AI could help them next.

The most useful thing I'd offer isn't a technique. It's a stance. In regulated domains, the interesting problem isn't making the AI better. It's designing the system around the AI so that when the AI is wrong, because it will be, sometimes, the failure is caught, the cost is bounded, and the trust doesn't collapse.

The AI product isn't the model. It's the system of controls, feedback, and human judgment that surrounds it. That's what determines whether AI in these domains earns the trust required to keep operating.
